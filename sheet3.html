Lecture 5 on one page: steam generators

What a steam generator contains

Furnace, superheater, reheater, boiler (evaporator), economizer, air preheater, plus burners, fans (FD, ID), dust collectors/ESP, ash handling, stack.

Applications

  • Utility: power plants. Subcritical drum type 130–180 bar; supercritical once-through ≥ 240 bar; 540–560 °C, 1–2 reheats; 120–1300 kg/s
  • Industrial: 5–105 bar, up to 125 kg/s, wet/saturated steam or hot water
  • Marine: ≈ 60–65 bar, 540 °C

Fire-tube vs water-tube

  • Fire-tube: gas inside tubes, water outside. Saturated steam, ≤ ~18 bar, ≤ 6.2 kg/s. Limited by shell stress σ = p·d/(2t)
  • Water-tube: water inside tubes, gas outside. Higher pressure and capacity at reasonable metal stress; bent-tube designs are easier to inspect and give drier steam

Heat absorption order

Economizer (heats to ≈ Tsat) → evaporator (downcomer-riser) → superheaters (> 40 % of heat in modern boilers).

Circulation: the part examiners like

Natural circulation

Δp = g·H·(ρd − ρr)
  • ρd = saturated water density in the downcomer
  • ρr = mean riser density ≈ (ρbottom + ρtop)/2, with ρbottom = ρf and ρtop = 1/(vf + xtop·vfg)
  • xtop is the top dryness fraction (TDF)

Circulation ratio

CR = ṁwater in downcomers / ṁsteam leaving drum = 1/TDF
  • CR must stay between 6 and 25 in any riser tube
  • Tubes facing the burners make more steam → lower CR
  • Density difference shrinks as pressure rises; at the critical pressure (221 bar) natural circulation is impossible
  • > 30 bar: downcomers outside the furnace. > 180 bar: forced circulation (pump)

Try it: natural-circulation head (live, from steam-table data)

Slide the pressure up: ρd − ρr collapses, so the available head drops even though H is fixed. That is exactly why Lecture 5 moves to forced circulation above ~180 bar.

Why the flue gas leaves at ≈ 160 °C

That is an availability loss, but it is accepted because (1) the gas must stay above the dew point, otherwise SO₂/SO₃ condense into acids that corrode metal; (2) the gas needs buoyancy to rise in a high plume above the stack. Combustion air is preheated to 250–400 °C by the outgoing gas in the air preheater.

Superheaters, reheaters and temperature control

Types

  • Convective: outlet T rises with load
  • Radiant: outlet T falls with load
  • Combined in series: the two trends cancel → flat response

Low steam temperature loses plant efficiency; high temperature risks failure of superheater/reheater tubes and turbine blades.

Eight control methods

  1. Radiant + convective in series
  2. Desuperheating/attemperation (spray)
  3. Gas by-pass / dampers
  4. Gas recirculation
  5. Excess air
  6. Tilting burners
  7. Burner selection
  8. Separately fired superheater

Air preheaters

Hot air stabilises combustion and cuts unburnt-carbon losses. Two types: recuperative (fixed surface) and regenerative (rotating matrix).

Self-test: 8 quick questions (tap to reveal answers)
  1. Which boiler type is limited by σ = p·d/2t, and why?
    Fire-tube: the shell is large-diameter and carries the full pressure.
  2. What is CR if the top dryness fraction is 0.1? 10, inside 6–25.
  3. At what pressure must you use a pump for circulation? Above ≈ 180 bar; natural circulation is impossible at 221 bar.
  4. Where do you place downcomers above 30 bar? Outside the furnace, to keep their water dense.
  5. Two reasons for 160 °C exit gas? Dew-point corrosion and plume buoyancy.
  6. Which superheater type gives a falling temperature with load? Radiant.
  7. What does the economizer do? Heats feed water to about Tsat using gas leaving the last superheater/reheater (370–540 °C).
  8. Name three ways to control superheat temperature. Spray attemperation, gas recirculation, tilting burners.

Closed boiler drum: fuel to raise the pressure

Exam hit: Finals 2026Solution on sheet

A 10 m³ boiler drum is 2⁄3 full of water (at 119.6 °C); the rest is dry saturated steam. The stop valve stays closed. How much fuel oil (CV = 41 800 kJ/kg, heat-transfer efficiency 90 %) is needed to raise the pressure to 8 bar? Find the final drum contents.

Given

  • V = 10 m³: Vw = 6.667 m³, Vs = 3.333 m³
  • T₁ = 119.6 °C (saturated) → p₁ ≈ 2 bar
  • p₂ = 8 bar, CV = 41 800 kJ/kg, η = 0.9

Find

  • Fuel mass mf
  • Final water and steam masses (and x₂)

Idea in one line

The valve is shut, so the drum is a rigid closed system: the mass m and the volume V cannot change. No work is done, so the heat added equals the rise in internal energy. Use u, never h.

Q = m·(u₂ − u₁) mf = Q / (CV·η) v₂ = v₁ = V/m

Steps

  1. State 1: split the drum into water and steam masses.Enter the saturation table at 119.6 °C (≈ 2 bar). Read vf = 0.001061 and vg = 0.8857 m³/kg.
    mw = Vw/vf = 6.667/0.001061 = 6,286.2 kg ms = Vs/vg = 3.333/0.8857 = 3.76 kg
    m = 6,290.0 kg x₁ = ms/m = 0.00060 (0.060 %)
  2. Internal energy at state 1. uf = 504.5, ug = 2529.1 kJ/kg at 2 bar.
    u₁ = uf + x₁(ug − uf) = 505.68 kJ/kg
  3. State 2 from the constant specific volume. v₂ = 10/6,290.0 = 0.001590 m³/kg. At 8 bar: vf = 0.001115, vg = 0.24033.
    x₂ = (v₂ − vf)/(vg − vf) = 0.00199
    uf = 720.1, ug = 2576.0 at 8 bar →
    u₂ = 723.81 kJ/kg
  4. Heat and fuel.
    Q = 6,290.0×(723.81 − 505.68) = 1,372,027 kJ
    mf = 1,372,027/(41 800×0.90) = 36.47 kg
  5. Final contents.
    msteam = x₂·m = 12.49 kg mwater = 6,277.5 kg
    The steam mass rises from 3.76 to 12.49 kg: almost all the heat goes into warming the water.

Final answer

  • mf ≈ 36.5 kg of fuel oil (your solution sheet: 36.43 kg)
  • Final drum: 6,277.5 kg water + 12.5 kg dry-sat. steam, x₂ ≈ 0.20 %, T₂ = 170.4 °C
Where marks are lost

Using h instead of u. Using x₂ = Vs/V (volume fraction) instead of the mass fraction. Forgetting that the steam volume is a third of the drum but its mass is only 0.06 % of the content.

Table tips

If your table gives only h, use u = h − p·v (p in kPa, v in m³/kg). Tables differ slightly (119.6 °C is listed for ~2 bar in some books, 120.2 °C in others): the exam marks the method, and answers within a few % are fine.

Exam variant (Finals 2026): 120 °C, final pressure 8.4 bar

Same method. At 120 °C the table gives psat = 1.987 bar. For 8.4 bar interpolate between the 8 bar and 9 bar rows (or use a table that lists 8.4 bar). Result: mf ≈ 38.1 kg, x₂ ≈ 0.21 %, final contents ≈ 6 278 kg water + 13.0 kg steam.

Furnace exit gas temperature and heat release rate

Method from Lecture 5No official solution

The heat-absorbing surface of a furnace is 11 500 m². It receives combustion air at 120 °C and burns liquid fuel at 300 ton/hr. Air and flue-gas rates are 11.8 and 12.7 kg per kg fuel. HHV = 42 000 kJ/kg. Find the furnace-exit gas temperature if 50 % of the heat available in the fuel is absorbed by the water tubes. What is the heat release rate based on the surface area?

Not in the solutions you uploaded

No official solution exists for this one. The data omit specific heats and a reference temperature, so the guide states them as assumptions; on the exam, write the assumptions down, that is what earns the marks.

Assumptions to write

  • cp,air = 1.005, cp,gas = 1.13 kJ/kg·K (the value used in Sheet 3 Q6)
  • Reference temperature = ambient 25 °C (sensible heat is counted above it)
  • Fuel enters at 25 °C; no losses other than the flue gas
  1. Flows.
    mf = 300×1000/3600 = 83.33 kg/s mair = 11.8 mf = 983.3 kg/s mg = 12.7 mf = 1058.3 kg/s
  2. Heat released by the fuel.
    Qf = mf·HHV = 83.33×42 000 = 3,500,000 kW
  3. Heat absorbed by the water tubes = 0.5 Qf = 1,750,000 kW.
  4. Sensible heat brought in by the hot air.
    Qair = 983.3×1.005×(120 − 25) = 93,884 kW
  5. Energy balance on the furnace. Everything not absorbed leaves with the gas:
    mg·cp,g·(Texit − 25) = 0.5 Qf + Qair
    Texit = 25 + (1,750,000 + 93,884) / (1058.3×1.13) = 1567 °C
  6. Heat release rate on the surface.
    HRR = Qf/A = 3,500,000/11 500 = 304.3 kW/m²

Final answer

  • Texit ≈ 1,567 °C (≈1 563 °C if you take the reference at 0 °C: the choice moves the answer by only about 4 °C)
  • Heat release rate ≈ 304 kW/m² (≈ 313 kW/m² if the air's sensible heat is counted as released heat)
Sanity check

Real furnace-exit gas is about 1 000–1 300 °C. A value above 1 500 °C comes from the very high 50 % absorption figure in the sheet; it is not an error in the method.

Forced-circulation pump power

Solution on sheetLecture 5: circulation

A drum-type steam generator operates at 160 bar. 1260 kg/s of water goes down the downcomer, 7.32 °C subcooled. Downcomer and riser are 12 m high. The average water/steam density in the riser is 350 kg/m³. The pressure loss in downcomer and riser is 0.5 bar. Find the power to drive a forced-recirculation pump of efficiency 0.7.

Concept

Heavy water falls in the downcomer, light mixture rises in the riser. The density difference gives a driving head (circulation head, CH). If friction needs more than CH, a pump supplies the shortfall.

Equations

CH = (ρd − ρr)·g·H
Δppump = Δploss − CH
P = Δppump·V̇ / ηpump, V̇ = ṁ·v
  1. Downcomer water state. Tsat(160 bar) = 347.4 °C → T = 347.4 − 7.32 = 340.0 °C (≈ 340 °C). Water is compressed liquid at 160 bar, 340 °C.
    Table: there is no compressed-liquid table for this point in most books, so use saturated liquid at 340 °C: vf ≈ 0.0016 m³/kg. (Exact value: v = 0.001617, ρd = 618.6 kg/m³.)
  2. Circulation head.
    CH = (618.6 − 350)×9.81×12 = 31,615 Pa = 31.6 kPa
  3. Pump pressure rise.
    Δppump = 50 000 − 31,615 = 18,385 Pa
  4. Volume flow in the downcomer.
    V̇ = 1260×0.00162 = 2.037 m³/s
  5. Pump power.
    P = 18,385×2.037/0.7 = 53,498 W = 53.5 kW

Final answer

  • Ppump ≈ 53 kW (your sheet: 50.76 kW with v = 0.0016; the small gap is only the table value of v)
Common slip

Taking ρd as the saturated water density at 160 bar (347 °C). The problem says the water is subcooled by 7.32 °C, which is exactly how you find its temperature, hence its density.

Steam generator: furnace volume, fuel rate, furnace-exit gas

Needs combustionNo official solution

A steam generator produces 250 ton/hr steam at 80 bar, 540 °C from feed water at 60 °C. Air at 25 °C, 14 % excess. Liquid fuel by mass: C 0.85, H₂ 0.135, O₂ 0.015. HHV = 44 000, LHV = 40 000 kJ/kg. On HHV the boiler efficiency is 86 %, net heat release rate 1 400 000 kJ/hr·m³ of furnace volume. Energy absorbed by furnace-wall tubes = 40 % of the net heat released. Estimate (a) furnace volume, (b) fuel rate, (c) temperature of gases leaving the furnace.

Not in the solutions you uploaded

Solved here from the lecture method. The order that works: (b) fuel first → (a) volume → (c) gas temperature.

Assumptions to write

  • Air = 23.2 % O₂ and 76.8 % N₂ by mass; no moisture in air; no ash
  • Feed-water enthalpy = hf at 60 °C (pump work ignored)
  • cp,gas = 1.13 kJ/kg·K (course value); gas leaving the furnace is measured above 25 °C (air enters at 25 °C, so the air brings no extra sensible heat)
  1. Steam heat duty (steam tables). h(80 bar, 540 °C) = 3497.5 kJ/kg (superheated table). hf(60 °C) = 251.2 kJ/kg.
    Qs = 69.44×(3497.5 − 251.2) = 225,439 kW
  2. (b) Fuel rate. Efficiency is on HHV:
    mf = Qs/(η·HHV) = 225,439/(0.86×44 000) = 5.958 kg/s = 21.4 t/h
  3. (a) Furnace volume. The net heat release rate uses the LHV:
    Qnet = mf·LHV = 5.958×40 000 = 238,308 kW = 857,907,930 kJ/h
    V = 857,907,930/1 400 000 = 613 m³
  4. Combustion: air and gas per kg fuel.
    ItemCalculationkg per kg fuel
    O₂ for C0.85 × 32/122.2667
    O₂ for H₂0.135 × 81.0800
    minus O₂ in fuel−0.015
    Theoretical O₂3.3317
    Theoretical air÷ 0.23214.361

    Check by species: CO₂ 3.117 + H₂O 1.215 + N₂ 12.573 + excess O₂ 0.466 = 17.371.
  5. (c) Gas temperature leaving the furnace. Gas flow = 5.958×17.371 = 103.5 kg/s. 40 % of Qnet goes to the walls; the other 60 % heats the gas:

HHV vs LHV

Boiler efficiency here is quoted on the higher heating value, so the fuel rate uses HHV. The heat release rate is called "net", so the volume uses LHV. Mixing them up changes the answer by about 10 %.

Spray de-superheating: maximum spray and peak temperature

Partly solved on sheetLecture 5: temperature control

The superheater characteristic is T = 220 + ṁs/82 (T in °C, ṁs in kg/hr). Steam pressure 22 bar. Spray de-superheating keeps the temperature constant over 7 to 11 t/hr of steam. Spray water is at 95 °C. Find the maximum spray water needed and the highest steam temperature reached before de-superheating.

Idea

The superheater runs hotter as load rises. Spray can only cool, so the target temperature is the lowest natural temperature in the range (at 7 t/h). At every higher flow the sprayer removes the excess. The worst case is the highest flow, 11 t/h.

  1. Controlled outlet temperature (at 7 t/h).
    Tout = 220 + 7000/82 = 305.37 °C
  2. Highest temperature before de-superheating (at 11 t/h).
    Tmax = 220 + 11 000/82 = 354.15 °C
  3. Enthalpies at 22 bar (superheated table; interpolate between 300 and 350 °C/400 °C rows).
    PointWhereh (kJ/kg)
    Hot steam22 bar, 354.1 °C3142.7
    Target steam22 bar, 305.37 °C3031.1
    Spray waterhf at 95 °C398.0
  4. Mixing balance at 11 t/h (the sprayer is an adiabatic mixer at 22 bar):
    ṁs·hs + ṁw·hw = (ṁs + ṁw)·hout
    ṁw = ṁs(hs − hout)/(hout − hw) = 11 000×(3142.7 − 3031.1)/(3031.1 − 398.0) = 466 kg/h

Final answer

  • Highest steam temperature before de-superheating = 354.1 °C (at 11 t/h)
  • Maximum spray water ≈ 466 kg/h ≈ 0.130 kg/s (your sheet left the final number blank: its enthalpies 3142, 3031 and 399 give the same ≈ 464 kg/h)

Do not set the controlled temperature at the 11 t/h value: at 7 t/h the steam is only 305 °C and a sprayer cannot heat it up.

Economizer + air preheater: areas and capacity

Exam hit: Finals 2023Solution on sheet

In a steam generator with an economizer and an air preheater: gas into the economizer 700 °C; water in 80 °C, out 180 °C; air in 25 °C, out 200 °C; fuel 3.6 ton/hr; air/fuel = 17; cp,gas = 1.13 kJ/kg·K; Uecon = 45, UAPH = 30 W/m²K. Counter-flow in both. Find both surface areas and the steam generator capacity.

Assumption you must state

The sheet gives no exit gas temperature. Your instructor's solution assumes the gas leaves the air preheater at 200 °C. (The Fall 2023 final gives 150 °C instead, solved below.)

Order of attack

Start with the air preheater: its heat is fixed by the air (known flow, known temperatures). That gives the gas temperature between the two exchangers, which is the missing piece for the economizer.

  1. Flows. ṁf = 3.6/3.6 = 1 kg/s; ṁair = 17 kg/s; ṁgas = ṁair + ṁf = 18 kg/s.
  2. Air preheater duty (cp,air = 1.0035):
    QAPH = 17×1.0035×(200 − 25) = 2,985.4 kW
  3. Gas temperature between the exchangers.
    QAPH = ṁgcp,g(Tg2 − 200) → Tg2 = 200 + 2,985.4/(18×1.13) = 346.8 °C
  4. APH area (counter-flow LMTD). ΔT₁ = 346.8 − 200 = 146.8; ΔT₂ = 200 − 25 = 175.
    LMTD = (28.2)/ln(175/146.8) = 160.5 °C
    AAPH = Q/(U·LMTD) = 2,985,412/(30×160.5) = 620 m²
  5. Economizer duty.
    Qe = 18×1.13×(700 − 346.8) = 7,185 kW
  6. Water flow = steam generator capacity (cp,w = 4.18):
    ṁw = 7,185/(4.18×(180 − 80)) = 17.2 kg/s
  7. Economizer area. ΔT₁ = 700 − 180 = 520 (hot end); ΔT₂ = 346.8 − 80 = 266.8 (cold end).
    LMTD = (520 − 266.8)/ln(520/266.8) = 379.4 °C
    Ae = 7,184,587/(45×379.4) = 421 m²

Final answers match your instructor's sheet

  • Aeconomizer ≈ 421 m² (sheet: 420.76)
  • Aair preheater ≈ 620 m² (sheet: 620)
  • Steam generator capacity ≈ 17.2 kg/s (sheet: 17.2)
Fall 2023 final version: exit gas 150 °C, fuel 4 t/h, cp,g = 1.15

Same steps. ṁf = 1.111 kg/s, ṁair = 18.89, ṁg = 20.00 kg/s. QAPH = 3,317 kW → Tg2 = 150 + Q/(ṁgcp) = 294.2 °C. Qe = 9,333 kW → ṁw = 22.3 kg/s. LMTDs 344.8 and 108.9 °C → Ae ≈ 601 m², AAPH ≈ 1015 m², capacity ≈ 22.3 kg/s.

Feasibility check

Counter-flow means the gas enters the APH at its hot end (next to the 200 °C air outlet) and leaves at the cold end (next to the 25 °C air inlet). So Tg2 must exceed 200 °C and Tg3 must exceed 25 °C: both hold here. A lower assumed Tg3 shrinks the LMTD and makes the APH bigger.

Superheater design: area, tubes, passes, metal temperature

Open-ended designNo official solution

A steam generator produces superheated steam of 440 ton/hr at 127 bar, 535 °C. Flue gases enter the superheater at 1090 °C with 545 ton/hr. Estimate the superheater surface area, number of tubes and number of passes. Tube OD 6.25 cm, wall 6.35 mm, centre-to-centre 17.80 cm, in-line. Design steam flux 618 kg/s·m², design gas flux 2.1 kg/s·m², U = 18 W/m²K. Pendant type; sketch it; estimate the maximum metal temperature if the steam-side coefficient is 180 W/m²K.

Not in the solutions you uploaded

This is an open-ended design question and the sheet gives no worked answer. The method below uses only the data in the statement. It is the most open problem in the sheet, so the examiner will mark the method and the assumptions more than the final digits.

Assumptions to write

  • Steam enters the superheater as dry saturated steam at 127 bar, leaves at 535 °C
  • cp,gas = 1.13 kJ/kg·K; overall counter-flow LMTD
  • Steam-side tubes: inside diameter di = 62.5 − 2×6.35 = 49.8 mm
  • One tube-path per transverse pitch position, so the gas free-flow area is Nt(ST − do)H
  1. Heat duty. hg(127 bar) = 2669.9, h(127 bar, 535 °C) = 3435.1 kJ/kg (interpolate 120/130 bar).
    Q = 122.22×(3435.1 − 2669.9) = 93,524 kW
  2. Gas outlet temperature. ṁg = 151.39 kg/s.
    ΔTg = 93,524/(151.39×1.13) = 547 K → Tg,out = 543 °C
  3. LMTD (Tsat(127 bar) = 329.0 °C at the steam inlet): ΔThot end = 1090 − 535 = 555; ΔTcold end = 543 − 329 = 214.
    LMTD = (555 − 214)/ln(555/214) = 358 °C
  4. Surface area.
    A = Q/(U·LMTD) = 93,523,700/(18×358) = 14,513 m²
  5. Number of tubes (steam side). Steam flow area = ṁs/Gs = 122.22/618 = 0.1978 m². Flow area per tube = π/4·di² = 19.478 cm².
    Nt = 0.1978/0.001948 = 101.5 → 102 tubes in parallel
  6. Tube length per pass from the gas side. Gas free-flow area = ṁg/Gg = 151.39/2.1 = 72.1 m².
    Ag = Nt(ST − do)·H → H = 72.1/(102×(0.178 − 0.0625)) = 6.12 m
  7. Number of passes. Total tube length per path = A/(Nt·π·do) = 725 m →
    passes = 725/6.12 ≈ 118
    A very large number because U = 18 W/m²K is low; say so in the answer and note that a higher U or longer tubes shrink it.
  8. Maximum metal temperature (hot end: gas 1090 °C meets steam at 535 °C). Wall resistance and area ratio ignored, hi = 180:
    q″ = U(Tg − Ts) = 18×(1090 − 535) = 9 990 W/m²
    Tm,max = Ts + q″/hi = 535 + 9 990/180 = 590.5 °C

Final answer

  • Q ≈ 93.5 MW, gas leaves at ≈ 543 °C, LMTD ≈ 358 °C
  • A ≈ 14,513 m²
  • 102 tubes in parallel, ≈ 6.1 m per pass, ≈ 118 passes (rows deep)
  • Tmetal,max ≈ 590 °C at the hot end
Marks to protect

The sketch (pendant tubes hanging from the roof, gas crossing horizontally, steam in at the cool end) and the maximum-metal-temperature location (steam outlet / gas inlet end) are cheap marks. Say why the hottest metal is there.

Riser tube metal temperature: normal vs steam-bound

Links to circulation (Lecture 5)No official solution

A 0.05 m outside-diameter riser tube in a convection pass is swept by combustion gas at 870 °C, gas-side film coefficient 56 W/m²K. The boiling water inside is at 315 °C with film coefficient 11 200 W/m²K. For this pressure a tube wall thickness of 0.005 m is required. Determine the average tube-metal temperature (disregarding tube-metal resistance) for normal steaming and for steam-bound (dry-out) conditions.

Not in the solutions you uploaded

The statement gives no inside coefficient for the steam-bound case. State an assumption. Here the steam-film coefficient of Q7 (180 W/m²K) is used and two more values are shown so you see the trend.

  1. Geometry. do = 0.05 m, di = 0.05 − 2×0.005 = 0.04 m. Per metre of tube the outer area is π·0.05 and the inner area π·0.04: that is why the thickness is given.
  2. Thermal resistances per metre (wall resistance ignored → one metal temperature):
    Rg = 1/(56·π·0.05) = 0.1137 K·m/W Ri = 1/(11 200·π·0.04) = 0.000711 K·m/W
  3. Heat flow per metre.
    q = (870 − 315)/(Rg + Ri) = 4,851.7 W/m
  4. Metal temperature (normal steaming).
    Tm = Ts + q·Ri = 315 + 4851.7×0.000711 = 318.4 °C
    A shortcut that ignores the area ratio gives (56×870 + 11 200×315)/(56 + 11 200) = 317.8 °C. Same story.
  5. Steam-bound (dry-out). The inside is now a thin steam film with hi ≈ 180 W/m²K:
    Ri = 1/(180·π·0.04) = 0.0442 → Tm = 470 °C
Inside coefficient h_i (W/m²K)ConditionTube metal T (°C)

Final answer

  • Normal steaming: ≈ 318 °C (only 3 °C above the water: the water cools the tube)
  • Steam-bound: ≈ 470 °C with hi = 180; the metal is 150 °C hotter, which can overheat and rupture the tube
The one-sentence moral

The metal temperature is a weighted average of the gas and fluid temperatures, weighted by the film coefficients. A big inside coefficient pins the metal near the fluid; lose it (dry-out) and the metal drifts toward the gas.
